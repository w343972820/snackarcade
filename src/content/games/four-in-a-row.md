  intro: >-
    Four in a Row is the classic drop-the-disc game that takes ten seconds to learn and a
    lifetime to master. Two players take turns dropping coloured discs into a seven-column
    grid, and the first to line up four of their own discs — horizontally, vertically or
    diagonally — wins. It is the perfect two-player game for one keyboard or one phone,
    and the computer opponent makes it just as good solo. Play it free in your browser
    with no download and no account.

  about:
    - >-
      The drop-disc game has been a family favourite since the 1970s, prized for its simple
      rules and surprisingly deep strategy. A full game rarely lasts more than a few
      minutes, which makes it ideal for quick sessions. Despite the simple rules, the game
      has real depth: the first player can force a win with perfect play, which is exactly
      why the computer opponent is worth taking seriously.
    - >-
      The board is seven columns wide and six rows tall. On each turn you drop a disc into
      a column, where it falls to the lowest empty space. Because gravity controls where
      the disc lands, you are not just choosing a square — you are choosing a column, and
      every disc you drop changes the shape of the whole grid for both players.
    - >-
      This version is based on the open-source implementation by Kenrick Chien, released
      under the MIT licence. It runs entirely in your browser with a computer opponent for
      solo play and a local two-player mode for sharing one screen. No download, no
      sign-up and no waiting for a matchmaking server.

        Pick a game against the computer or a two-player game on the same device. In

    - step: "Drop a disc into a column."
      detail: >-
        On your turn, choose one of the seven columns. Your disc falls to the lowest empty
        space in that column, so a column with no discs lets you play the bottom row.
    - step: "Line up four discs."
      detail: >-
        The first player to line up four of their own discs in a straight line — across,
        down or diagonally — wins the game immediately.
    - step: "Block your opponent."
      detail: >-
        If the opponent has three discs lined up with an open end, that open end is a
        winning threat. Blocking those threats is the entire defensive half of the game.
    - step: "Win or reset."
      detail: >-
        The game ends when someone connects four or when the grid is full with no winner,
        which is a draw. Start a new round with the New Game button.

  tips:
    - title: "Win the middle column"
      body: >-
        The centre column participates in the most possible winning lines. Controlling it
        early gives you more routes to four in a row and makes it harder for your opponent
        to build their own.
    - title: "Build threats two ways at once"
      body: >-
        A single disc that creates two separate three-in-a-row threats is almost impossible
        to block, because your opponent can only cover one. Look for columns that set up
        double threats.
    - title: "Play underneath, not on top"
      body: >-
        Because discs fall to the bottom, a disc in a low row is never covered by the
        opponent. Prefer placing your discs under open columns rather than stacking on top
        of the opponent's discs.
    - title: "Block every three before it becomes four"
      body: >-
        The moment the opponent has three in a row with an open end, that end is a winning
        move. Check for those threats after every one of your moves, before you start your
        own attack.
    - title: "Count the columns before you commit"
      body: >-
        A column can only hold six discs. When a column is nearly full, the move you play
        there might be your last chance to use it — plan for the column running out.
    - title: "Sacrifice a column to force the opponent"
      body: >-
        Sometimes playing a disc in a column that does not help you directly forces your
        opponent into a bad response. A quiet move that keeps your threats alive is often
        stronger than a flashy one.
