# Reversi Strategy: How to Win the Disc-Flipping Game

Reversi looks like the simplest game on the shelf: place a disc, flip whatever is trapped in a line, and finish with the bigger pile. That surface hides one of the deepest two-player board games there is — a game where the scoreboard lies to you, where the best move often flips the fewest discs, and where the last five moves matter more than the first thirty. This guide covers the strategy strong players actually use, and you can try all of it on the [free Reversi board here](/games/reversi/) against a friend on one screen.

## How the Game Flows

Reversi is played on an eight by eight board that starts with four discs — two of each colour — in the centre, with black moving first. On your turn, place a disc on an empty square so it flanks an unbroken line of your opponent's discs between your new disc and one of your own, in any of the eight directions; every disc in that line flips to your colour. With no legal move you pass, and when neither player can move the game ends. The [Reversi game page](/games/reversi/) has the full rules and controls.

## Corners Are Permanent

A corner disc can never be flipped. It sits on two outer edges, and no line can pass through it to flank from beyond, because there is no square beyond a corner. Every other disc can change hands; a corner disc stays your colour to the end — which is why corners, not the disc count, are the true currency of Reversi.

Corners also unlock the edges. An edge held between two corners is nearly impossible to attack, and a defended corner projects control across half the board. Take a corner the moment it is safe, and spend the rest of the game denying your opponent one.

## X-Squares and C-Squares: The Squares That Give Away a Corner

Two squares near every corner are traps. The **X-square** is the square diagonally adjacent to a corner; the **C-square** is the edge square immediately beside it, one per edge.

Playing either usually gifts the opponent the corner. Put a disc on the X-square beside a corner and you have placed it on that corner's own diagonal: if your opponent already has a disc further along the diagonal, they can now play the corner, and their move flanks through your X-square disc and flips it. One move, and the corner — the one disc that can never be taken back — is theirs. A disc on the C-square attacks the corner from the side in much the same way.

Treat every X-square and C-square as off-limits early, unless playing there takes the corner immediately. Use the table below as a quick reference.

| Square | Where it is | How to treat it | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Corner | A board corner | Take it whenever safe | Can never be flipped |
| X-square | Diagonally next to a corner | Avoid unless it wins the corner now | Your disc there lets the opponent take the corner |
| C-square | Edge square beside a corner | Avoid early | It leaks the corner and can lock the edge |
| Edge | Any outer edge square | Play once a corner backs it | A bare edge strip is a target |
| Centre | The middle rows and columns | Safe and flexible | The most flanking lines and mobility |

## Mobility Is the Real Objective

Disc count in the middle game is a distraction; what matters is **mobility** — how many legal moves each side has. A position where you have eight options and your opponent has two is winning whatever the counter says, because you are dictating and they are reacting.

So judge each move by a different question than "how many discs does it flip?". Ask "how many moves do I leave my opponent?". Squeezing those options is the whole middle game. When a player runs out of safe moves they are pushed into the X-squares and C-squares, and one bad move near a corner often loses the board.

## Why Being Behind Can Be Winning

The counter-intuitive heart of Reversi is that in the middle game you are often better off behind. Fewer discs usually means your discs are grouped and compact, which leaves more empty squares and therefore more legal moves for you and fewer for your opponent. A big early lead looks impressive and is frequently a death sentence, because those discs are scattered and easy to flip back in bulk.

Abandon the instinct to flip as much as possible. The player who grabs the disc lead early often hands the opponent easy, high-value moves later. Play for structure and let the count look after itself — it only truly matters on the final move.

## Edges Are Strong — But Only When Backed

An edge full of your discs is powerful, but not all edges are equal. One anchored by both corners is close to permanent: it cannot be flanked from the ends, and its discs are among your most stable. An edge with no corner behind it is a liability in disguise — a bare strip gives your opponent targets on the row behind it, and every disc there can be flipped the moment they gain access along the adjacent line.

So commit to an edge only once a corner secures one end of it. If you must play one, prefer its middle over the squares next to the corners, and never open the C-square beside a corner you are keeping.

## Stable Discs: What the Endgame Actually Counts

A **stable disc** can never be flipped again, whatever happens later, and stability builds in layers. Corners are stable the moment they are played. Edge discs become stable once their corner anchors are yours. Discs in the second row become stable once the edge beside them is locked. Everything else is unstable until the board is nearly full.

That is why the endgame feels upside-down: the player with the fewest discs going into the last few moves often finishes with the most stable ones. Count the discs that are yours for good, not the discs that are yours for now.

## Parity: Counting Empty Squares to the End

Over roughly the last ten empty squares, stop counting discs and start counting space. The player who makes the last move in a region flips everything in it and gives no reply there — a free swing. So the endgame question is not "what flips most?" but "who plays last in each pocket?".

Practically: count the empty squares in each region and note whether the number is odd or even; an odd count means the player to move can be the one to play last there. You are not doing algebra, just tallying who gets the final word, and steering your turn order so that as many of those final words as possible are yours.

## Opening Principles

The opening is largely settled ground, and the first few moves are close to forced, so it is less about clever decisions than about habits. Two pay off immediately. First, refuse to chase the disc count — the player who runs ahead in the first twenty moves is usually the one losing. Second, keep away from every X-square and C-square until a corner is genuinely on offer. Do those two things and you reach the middle game intact, with more mobility than an opponent who treated the opening as a race.

## The Passing Rule Is a Weapon

A player with no legal move must pass, and if both are stuck the game ends. That rule is a tool, not just bookkeeping: if you can build a position where your opponent has no legal move while you still have one, you effectively move twice — they sit out, you play, and the board shifts before they react. Engineering a forced pass is one of the cleanest ways to win a tight game, and it usually comes from mobility pressure. Watch for the moment your opponent's legal moves shrink to one or two; that is your cue to find a move that removes them entirely.

## The First Five Habits to Build

1. **Take corners when they are safe.** A corner is the most valuable thing on the board — and do not give one away by playing beside it carelessly.
2. **Never play an X-square early.** The square diagonally next to a corner is almost never worth it until it wins the corner outright.
3. **Count mobility, not discs.** Before each move, check how many options it leaves your opponent, not how many discs it flips.
4. **Keep your discs grouped.** Compact, connected discs are harder to flip and leave you more moves.
5. **Count empty squares in the endgame.** In the last ten moves, tally the empty regions and fight to play last in as many as you can.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Are corners really the most important thing?**
Yes. A corner disc can never be flipped, so it is permanent, and it anchors the edges beside it. Strong players will accept a worse disc count in the middle game to secure one.

**What is an X-square?**
The square diagonally adjacent to a corner. Playing there usually hands the corner to your opponent, because your disc becomes the piece they flip when they take it. Avoid it unless taking the corner is your next move.

**Should I flip as many discs as I can each turn?**
No — this is the most common beginner mistake. Flipping a lot early usually gives your opponent more legal moves and leaves your own discs exposed. Prefer compact moves that keep your options open.

**Is Reversi the same game as Othello?**
The rules are effectively the same; the names differ. Reversi is the older public-domain name, usually traced to nineteenth-century England, while "Othello" is the name used by the trademarked commercial version, commonly credited to Gabriel Industries in the 1970s. This site uses the public-domain name.
