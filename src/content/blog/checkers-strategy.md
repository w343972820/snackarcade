# Checkers Strategy: How to Win the Diagonal Game

Checkers has a reputation problem. Everyone learned it as a child, so everyone assumes they already know it — and then they lose to someone who understands that the board is a one-way escalator, that a captured piece is often the cheapest thing you can spend, and that the player who reaches the far row first usually wins. This guide covers the strategy that separates "I know the rules" from "I win games", and you can try all of it on the [free Checkers board here](/games/checkers/) against a friend on one screen.

## How a Game of Checkers Flows

Checkers is played on the dark squares of an eight by eight board. Each side starts with twelve men — red moves first, then players alternate. A man moves one square diagonally forward onto an empty dark square. It captures by jumping diagonally over an adjacent enemy man onto the empty square beyond, and if another jump is available from the square it lands on, the jump continues in the same turn.

You win by capturing every enemy man, or by leaving your opponent with no legal move at all. That second condition matters more than beginners expect: a player with three men and no move loses to a player with one man who can still move.

This version runs with a few options you can toggle, and it is worth knowing the defaults: the board size defaults to eight, **captures are forced**, men capture forwards only, and kings run any distance along an open diagonal. The strategy below assumes those defaults.

## Captures Are Forced — Plan Around It

The single most important rule for a new player to internalise is that **you cannot decline a capture**. If a jump exists, you must take it. Experienced players do not fight this rule; they use it.

A forced capture turns your opponent into a machine. Put a man on a square where the jump looks free, and they have to jump it — even when the landing square hands you two men back. That is the shot, the sacrifice that wins games, and it only exists because captures are compulsory.

So before you move, ask two questions in this order: *does my opponent have a capture after this move?* And *if they take it, what can I take next?* Most games between beginners are decided by who asked the second question first — and the fastest way to build that reflex is to set up a bait piece on the [two-player Checkers game](/games/checkers/) and watch the forced reply happen without you.

## Men Move Forward Only

A man never retreats. It moves and captures diagonally forward, one square at a time. That makes the board a one-way escalator: every move you make commits a piece further up the board, and it can never come back down.

Two consequences follow. First, the far side of the board is where power lives — which is why promotion is not a bonus but a plan. Second, a man that has climbed too far up on the edge is nearly worthless: it has one legal move, and taking it hands the square to your opponent. Pushing a man forward is a decision, not a habit.

## The Centre Is Worth More Than the Edge

A piece is only as good as the squares it can reach. A man in the middle of the board sits on squares with two forward options; a man on an edge square has one. That difference compounds over thirty moves.

| Where the man stands | Forward moves available | How to treat it |
|---|---|---|
| Centre / inner file | Two, and often a choice of captures | Build here; this is where attacks start |
| Second file in from the edge | Two, but one leads off the board | Playable, weaker — a support piece |
| Edge file | One, and it is a dead end | Avoid unless you are trading or blocking |
| Back row | One, forward only | Keep it until you have to break it |

Contest the centre early. Games are usually lost on the wings and won in the middle.

## Build a Wall, Not a Scatter

A connected chain of men cannot be jumped through. To capture, your opponent needs an enemy man diagonally adjacent *and* the square beyond it empty — and in a solid diagonal formation, the square beyond is occupied by your next man. A wall is not a defensive luxury; it is the physical reason attacks fail.

A single gap, though, is a doorway. One hole in a diagonal line lets an enemy man land inside your formation, and from there it can often chain two or three jumps before it stops. When you see a hole forming in your own structure, fill it before you do anything ambitious.

The practical test: after every move, mentally draw the diagonals your men sit on. If any of them is broken with an enemy man adjacent to the gap, your opponent has a target.

## The Back Row Is a Fence

Beginners break the back row too early because a back-row man feels wasted — it has one move and it never takes anything. But the back row is the only thing standing between an enemy man and a king.

Every man that reaches your back row is crowned. So your back row is not a bench; it is a fence with a job. Break it only when you gain something concrete: a forced capture, a piece that stops an enemy breakthrough somewhere else, or the last few moves of an endgame where your own king is already home.

The most common beginner loss looks like this: they push the back row forward for tempo, an enemy man slips through the gap two moves later, and suddenly they are playing against a king.

## Kings Change the Board

A man that reaches the opponent's far row is crowned a king, and a king is a different animal. It moves diagonally in both directions — and in this version it also runs any number of empty squares along an open diagonal, the rule known in draughts as a free-running or flying king.

That combination is decisive. A king on an open diagonal controls the whole line: it can enter from either end, and it can reach a distant capture in one move that a man could never make. Rules of thumb worth remembering:

- A king is roughly worth three men in the middlegame, and far more in an endgame with open lines.
- One king against two or three men on a crowded board is often a draw, not a win — kings need space.
- Trade two men for a king only when the resulting position gives the king an open diagonal.

Get a king first, and the burden of proof shifts to your opponent.

## How to Trade Well

Every capture is a trade, and trades are where material is won and lost. Three rules carry most of the weight.

**Trade when you are ahead.** If you have more men than your opponent, exchanging pieces simplifies the position and makes your advantage easier to convert. Fewer pieces on the board means fewer chances for a clever shot to undo your lead.

**Count before you jump.** A capture that lands your man where the opponent can immediately take it is not a gain — it is a swap on their terms. Count the chain one move further than the obvious one.

**Do not chase material into a bad square.** A man that captures its way to the edge and then has nowhere to go has cost you mobility to win a piece you cannot use. Position first, material second.

## The Two-on-One Ending

The classic endgame is two men against one, and it teaches the whole logic of the game in miniature. The side with two men wants to force a trade in a corner where the single defender cannot recapture. The side with one man wants to reach the double corner, where a single man often holds a draw by shuttling between two squares.

If you are the stronger side: drive the lone man toward the *wrong* corner — the single corner, where it has only one escape route — and use your spare man to control the square behind it. If you are the weaker side: head for the double corner immediately, and never let yourself be pushed to the other one.

## Safe Squares and Guard Squares

Not all squares are equally dangerous. The vocabulary is worth learning because it turns a vague sense of threat into something you can check.

| Square type | What it means | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Guarded square | A friendly man sits diagonally behind it | A man here cannot be captured — it is defended from behind |
| Hole | A gap in your own diagonal line | An enemy man can land inside and chain jumps |
| Dog hole | A hole next to the edge | The most dangerous kind: it can start a run along the edge |
| Double corner | The two corner squares on the same side | The defender's fortress in endgames |
| Single corner | The corner with only one escape route | Where a lone defender gets trapped |

Learn to see guarded squares and holes, and most tactical mistakes disappear before you make them.

## Opening Ideas

There is no book of twenty openings to memorise here, but there are sound first moves.

Advance from the centre files rather than the wings — a man that steps off the edge file early has already spent its usefulness. Avoid jumping just because a jump is offered in the opening; a first capture that trades a mobile man for an edge square is usually a loss dressed as a win, even when it is forced. Keep the back row intact for at least the first ten moves. And watch the long diagonal: the opening is largely a race to see who controls it.

## Five Habits for Your Next Game

1. **Check their captures before your own.** After every candidate move, ask what your opponent can take.
2. **Keep the back row whole** until you have a concrete reason to break it.
3. **Prefer the centre.** Trade a wing man for a centre man whenever you can force it.
4. **Count one move deeper.** Do not stop at the first capture you see.
5. **Plan for a king.** Pick a man and commit to walking it home; do not drift.

Habits like these are the difference between playing a game and playing a position. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I choose not to jump?** In this version, no — captures are forced. If a jump exists, the board will only offer you capturing moves.

**Can a man capture backwards?** Not by default here. Men capture forwards only, which is the standard English draughts rule. The demo panel has a toggle that enables backward captures if you want to experiment.

**How many squares can a king move?** As far as it likes along an unobstructed diagonal, in either direction. Kings in this version are free-running, so an open diagonal is a highway.

**What happens if I cannot move?** You lose. Having no legal move is a loss condition even if you still have men on the board — this is why mobility matters as much as material.

**How do I get better quickly?** Play with the back row intact, count captures one move deeper than you think you need to, and review every game you lose by asking which single move created the hole your opponent exploited.
