# Sudoku Strategies for Beginners: Solve Your First Grid

Sudoku looks intimidating the first time you open one: a 9 by 9 grid with a scattering of digits and a wall of empty cells. The good news is that the rules are almost insultingly simple, and there are only a handful of techniques you need to solve easy and medium puzzles. You do not need to be good at maths — Sudoku uses no arithmetic at all. You only need to be patient and methodical. This guide teaches the exact order of techniques to use, and when you are ready you can [play Sudoku online](/games/sudoku/) right here on SnackArcade, free and with no download.

## The Only Rule You Need

Every row, every column and every 3 by 3 box must contain the digits 1 to 9 exactly once. That is the whole game. When you fill a cell, the digit you choose must not already appear in that cell's row, column or box. The starting digits are fixed clues, and a well-formed puzzle has exactly one solution. Everything you learn from here is just a way of applying that single rule more efficiently.

## Technique One: Scanning for Single Candidates

The fastest way to start is to scan the grid for cells with only one possible digit. Pick a row and look at which digits are already present. If a row already contains 1, 2, 4, 5, 7 and 8, then the empty cells in that row can only hold 3, 6 or 9. Now check each empty cell's column and box to eliminate more. When a cell is down to one possible digit, that digit is forced — fill it in immediately. Scanning rows, columns and boxes in turn will usually crack the first third of an easy puzzle on its own.

## Technique Two: Notes and Pencil Marks

As the puzzle gets harder, your head will not be able to hold all the possibilities. That is what notes mode is for. In the game on [the Sudoku page](/games/sudoku/), you can switch to notes mode and jot every candidate digit into each empty cell — exactly like pencil marks on paper. The rule for writing notes is simple: a digit goes in a cell only if it is not already present in that cell's row, column or box. The notes are not a crutch; they are the working memory of the puzzle, and no serious Sudoku player solves hard grids without them.

## Technique Three: Hidden Singles

Once your notes are filled in, look for hidden singles. A hidden single is a digit that can only go in one cell of a row, column or box, even though that cell has several other candidates written in it. For example, if the digit 5 can only fit in one cell of a row, then 5 goes there — no matter what other notes the cell carries. Hidden singles are the second most common technique after plain scanning, and hunting for them will unlock puzzles that feel stuck.

## Technique Four: Naked Pairs

When two cells in the same row, column or box contain the same two candidate digits and nothing else, those two cells form a naked pair. The two digits must go in those two cells, which means they cannot appear anywhere else in that unit. Cross those digits out of the other cells' notes and you will often reveal a new single. Naked pairs are the first of the "advanced" techniques, but they are easy to spot once you know what to look for.

## Build the Habit of the Three Checks

The single biggest beginner mistake is checking only the row and column and forgetting the box. A digit can fit a row and a column perfectly and still be impossible because its 3 by 3 box already contains it. Make a habit of checking all three constraints before you commit, and before you write a note. It takes two extra seconds and prevents most of the errors that force you to restart.

## Practice on the Right Difficulty

Do not start on expert. The game here has four levels, and Easy is designed for learning the flow. Play easy puzzles until scanning feels automatic, then move to Medium, where notes start to matter. When you can finish a medium grid without guessing, try Hard. Each level is a step up in the number of techniques you need, and jumping straight to the top teaches you frustration instead of Sudoku.

## Solve Your First Grid Today

Open [Sudoku](/games/sudoku/), pick Easy, and follow the order: scan for single candidates, write notes for every remaining cell, hunt hidden singles, then look for naked pairs. Check the row, the column and the box on every move. The first puzzle will take a while; the tenth will feel completely different. That is the whole pleasure of Sudoku — the grid gets faster the more you understand it.
