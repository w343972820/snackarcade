# Chess Basics for Beginners: How to Start Winning

Chess has a reputation for being hard, and it is — if you try to learn everything at once. But the actual entry point is small. You need to know how six pieces move, what checkmate means, and a handful of opening habits. That is enough to play real games, and more than enough to beat other beginners. The version on SnackArcade makes learning even easier: legal moves are highlighted when you select a piece, and illegal moves are rejected automatically, so you can [play chess online](/games/chess/) and learn by trying. This guide covers the essentials in the order you need them.

## The Board and the Goal

The board is eight squares by eight, and White moves first. The goal is to checkmate the opponent's king: put it under attack with no way to escape, block or capture the attacker. You win the moment checkmate happens, no matter how many pieces are left on the board. Everything you learn about piece movement and strategy is in service of that single goal.

## How Each Piece Moves

The rook moves any number of squares horizontally or vertically. The bishop moves any number of squares diagonally, so a bishop stays on one colour for the whole game. The queen combines both — any number of squares in any straight line, which makes her the most powerful piece. The knight moves in an L shape: two squares one way, then one square sideways. It is the only piece that can jump over others. The pawn moves forward one square, captures diagonally, and can move two squares on its first move. And the king moves one square in any direction — the most important piece on the board and the least mobile.

## The Three Special Rules

Three rules look strange at first. Castling is a single move where the king slides two squares toward a rook and the rook jumps over to the square beside the king — it happens once per game and tucks the king to safety. En passant is a pawn capture where a pawn that moved two squares can be captured by an adjacent pawn as if it had moved one. Promotion lets a pawn reaching the last row become any piece, almost always a queen. All three are enforced automatically on [the Chess page](/games/chess/), so you will see them work even before you fully understand them.

## Check, Checkmate and Stalemate

When your king is attacked, you are in check and must escape on the next move: move the king, block the attack, or capture the attacker. If none of those is possible, it is checkmate and the game is over. A special case is stalemate: when the player to move has no legal moves but is not in check, the game is a draw. Stalemate is a classic beginner trap — you can be completely winning and still draw because you left your opponent with no legal moves.

## Three Opening Habits That Win Games

Beginners do not need to memorise opening theory. They need three habits. First, control the centre: moves like pushing the king's pawn or the queen's pawn give your pieces room and fight for the four central squares. Second, develop your pieces: bring out the knights and bishops early, and do not move the same piece twice in the opening. Third, castle early: tucking the king behind a wall of pawns and connecting your rooks is the safest way to start the middle game. Players who follow these three habits beat players who push random pawns, every time.

## The Most Common Beginner Blunder

The fastest way to lose a chess game is to leave your pieces hanging — undefended and capturable. After every move you make and every move your opponent makes, ask two questions: what is under attack, and is anyone in check? Checking for threats before you commit a move catches most blunders before they happen. It sounds slow, but it is the habit every strong player has, and it will win you more games than any tactical trick.
