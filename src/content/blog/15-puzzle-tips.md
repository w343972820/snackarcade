## Why the 15 Puzzle Feels Random but Isn't

Here is the fact that surprises everyone: of all the ways to arrange fifteen tiles, **exactly half are mathematically impossible to solve**. The property is called *parity* — a board is solvable only if its "inversions" line up correctly with where the blank sits. That is why an old physical 15 puzzle, if someone popped the tiles out and reassembled them wrong, could be unsolvable forever.

## The Layered Method: Solve One Row, Lock It, Repeat

The reliable way to solve any sliding puzzle is the **layered method**: build the top row left to right, lock it, then treat the remaining board as a smaller puzzle and do it again.

1. **Place 1, 2, 3 in the top row**, left to right. These go in directly — slide each into its home slot.
2. **Slot 4 with the corner trick** (below). The last tile of a row is the awkward one; don't force it, set it up.
3. **Lock the top row.** Once 1–4 are placed, never move them again.
4. **Repeat on the next row** (5, 6, 7, 8), then the next, until only a 2×2 block remains.

The discipline that makes this work is simple: **a solved row is permanent.** Every move from here on stays inside the unsolved region. Trying to "fix everything at once" is what creates the mess; solving in layers is what prevents it.

## The Corner Rotation Trick (The Part That Stumps Beginners)

Placing the final two tiles of any row is the one move that doesn't work by direct sliding. If you try to push tile 3 and tile 4 straight into the last two spots, you knock the row apart. Instead, rotate them in as a pair:

- Put tile 3 in the spot where **4** belongs.
- Put tile 4 **below** where 3 belongs.
- Now rotate the three tiles in that corner (including the one currently sitting in 3's target) until both 3 and 4 fall into place together.

It feels like magic the first time. This same rotation is needed for the last two tiles of **every** row except the final one, so practising it on the top row pays off all the way down. ## Use Undo as a Safety Net, Not a Crutch

Most guides assume you solve on paper. On a screen you have a superpower they ignore: **unlimited undo**. Used well, it is a safety net that removes the fear of experimenting.

When you are unsure whether sliding a tile will help or wreck your almost-solved row, try it, look at the result, and undo if it was wrong. That is not cheating — it is exactly how a strong solver builds intuition, just accelerated. The line between tool and crutch is the same as always: undo to *learn* which branch works, not to brute-force a board you have already mangled. Practise the rotations on a free, no-pressure board, then carry the patterns into games where you choose not to undo.

## Never Break a Solved Row

This is the single most common beginner mistake, and it deserves its own rule. The moment a row or column is correct, **treat it as locked concrete.** Every slide after that happens *inside* the unsolved area.

Why it matters: a solved row takes maybe ten careful moves to build. One careless slide that "borrows" a tile from it can cost you thirty moves to rebuild — and usually spills into the next row too. Before any move, ask: "Does this touch a finished section?" If yes, find another path for the blank. Protecting your past work is half the battle.

## Five Habits That Separate Solvers From Guessers

Run this check before you slide:

1. **Am I protecting solved rows?** Never borrow from a finished section.
2. **Is the blank routed where I need it?** Move the empty space to the target *before* sliding the tile in, not after.
3. **Did I set up the corner rotation** for the last two tiles, instead of forcing them?
4. **Am I working in layers** (top row → next row → smaller puzzle), not all at once?
5. **Did I use undo to learn** a branch I was unsure about?

Guessers slide and hope. Solvers plan two or three moves ahead and let the layers do the work.

## Common Mistakes That Waste Dozens of Moves

- **Sliding the blank back and forth with no plan.** Every wasted slide is a move you will have to undo later. Route the empty space deliberately.
- **Breaking a solved row to fix a later one.** The rebuild costs more than the "shortcut" saved. Keep all action in the unsolved region.
- **Forcing the last two tiles straight in.** They need the rotation, not brute force. Set them up as a pair.
- **Starting on 4×4 cold.** The 3×3 teaches the method in minutes; skipping it makes 4×4 feel impossible.

Every one of these is a *habit*, which means every one is fixable with repetition.

You do not need marathon sessions. Fifteen minutes a day beats an hour once a week, because the method sticks through repeated, spaced practice. A routine that works:

- **Days 1–3:** 3×3 only. Focus on locking the top row and nailing the corner rotation.

- **Ongoing:** keep a one-line note per solve — "stuck because I broke the top row" or "won because I set up the rotation early" — and your own pattern shows up within a week.
