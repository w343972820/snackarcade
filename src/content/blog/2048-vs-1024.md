# 2048 vs 1024: What's the Difference?

If you have played any sliding number puzzle, you have probably heard both names. 1024 and 2048 belong to the same family: swipe a grid, matching numbers merge, and you chase a target tile. But they are different games with different personalities, and the differences explain a lot about why 2048 became the one everyone remembers. This post compares the two side by side and tells you which one to open on [Play 2048](/games/2048/) when you want the full experience.

## The Target Tile Changes Everything

The most obvious difference is in the name. In 1024, the goal is to build a tile that reads 1024. In 2048, you have to double that and reach 2048. That single change alters the whole shape of a game. Reaching 1024 takes a few careful minutes and feels achievable even for a new player. Reaching 2048 requires the same kind of careful play but for twice as long, which is why the community around 2048 developed real strategies — corner locking, descending rows, the three-direction rule — that barely matter in a shorter game. The higher target turned a casual distraction into a puzzle with depth.

## Same Rules, Different Pressure

Both games share the same core rules: a four by four grid, tiles slide as far as they can, equal tiles merge, and a new tile appears after every move. The pace is where they diverge. 1024 is a shorter, looser session — you can play it while waiting for something else without much planning. 2048 demands more sustained attention because the board fills up faster relative to your progress. Every move in 2048 is a decision about structure, not just a swipe. If you want a quick warm-up, the shorter game wins; if you want to think, the longer one is the better fit.

## Why 2048 Became the Name Everyone Knows

2048 was created by Gabriele Cirulli over a single weekend in March 2014 and released as open source under the MIT license. Within its first week it drew roughly four million visitors, and because the code was open, clones and spin-offs spread everywhere. 1024 came first and established the format, but 2048 is the game that popularised it and turned the merge puzzle into a genre of its own. That is why you can still play 2048 today, a decade later, while 1024 survives mostly as a footnote in the genre's history.
