# Mahjong Solitaire Tile Matching: How to Play and Win

Mahjong Solitaire is the quietest puzzle on the internet: a board piled high with decorated tiles, and one simple job — remove every tile by matching identical pairs. There is no timer, no opponent and no way to lose except running out of legal matches. The game looks like it is about memory, but it is really about planning: which pairs you take, and in what order, decides whether the board clears or locks itself. This guide explains the rules and the strategy, and you can [play Mahjong Solitaire online](/games/mahjong-solitaire/) right here, free and with no download.

## The Rules in One Paragraph

The board is built from the traditional mahjong tile set, stacked in a layout such as a tower or the classic turtle shape. You remove tiles in pairs, but only when both tiles are identical and both are free. A tile is free when nothing sits on top of it and at least one of its left or right sides is open. Clear every tile from the board and you win. If no legal match remains, the game is over — though a shuffle can often rescue you.

## What "Free" Really Means

The concept of a free tile is the heart of the game, and beginners misunderstand it all the time. A tile in the middle of the board with neighbours on both sides is not free, even if nothing sits on top of it — its sides are blocked. A tile on the edge of a row is free on one side. A tile on the top of a stack is free only if its sides are also open. Before you click any pair, check both conditions: no tile above, and at least one open side. The game usually highlights free tiles to help, but understanding the rule lets you plan rather than click.

## Why Order Matters

Here is the mistake that loses games: clearing the easy pairs first. Every match you make changes which tiles are free, and some matches unlock deep, buried tiles while others just tidy the surface. The tiles at the bottom of a stack are the ones that end games — if you leave a buried pair until last, you may find it has no legal match by the time you reach it. Good players look for the pairs that unlock the most, not the pairs that are easiest to see. Take the tiles that free up the board's interior first.

## Match the Hard Pairs First

The practical version of the ordering rule is simple: match the pairs that are hardest to reach first. A pair buried under three layers of tiles is a debt — the longer you leave it, the more it restricts your options. A pair sitting on the top edge will still be there later. So scan the board for pairs that are close to becoming trapped and take those before anything else. This single habit will noticeably increase how many boards you finish.

## Keep the Layout in Mind

Different layouts play differently. Small layouts are quick and forgiving, ideal for learning. The classic turtle layout and other large shapes are longer games with more traps, because more tiles are buried in the middle. The game on [the Mahjong Solitaire page](/games/mahjong-solitaire/) offers a wide range of layouts, so you can start small and work up. As you learn a layout, you start to remember where the dangerous buried pairs tend to hide, which makes you dramatically faster.

## Use Hints and Shuffle Honestly

When you are stuck, the game offers a hint that shows a legal pair, and a shuffle that rearranges the board. These are not cheats — they are part of the game, and using them wisely is a skill. Use a hint to find the pair you are missing rather than to confirm the obvious. Use shuffle only when you genuinely have no legal moves or when the board is clearly unwinnable in its current arrangement, because shuffling erases the plan you have built. A good player treats hints as a teaching tool and shuffle as a last resort.
